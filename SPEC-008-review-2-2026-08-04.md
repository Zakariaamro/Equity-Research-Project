# Second review — what stands between this and a professional tool

**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Reviewed:** localhost:8502, all four pages, all three companies, both bases, all three statements
**Status:** Findings only. Nothing designed here.

The first review was about things that were broken. Most of those are fixed. This one is
about the gap between "correct" and "good enough for someone who does this for a living" —
which is a different and harder problem, and it is mostly **not** a visual-design problem.

Two things dominate everything else below:

1. **A quarter of the income statement is missing.** The discrete-quarter derivation was
   built for cash flow only. Q4 is blank for every company, every year, on the income
   statement — verified in the data, not inferred.
2. **There is no year-over-year comparison anywhere in the quarterly tables.** Only
   sequential. For a business with any seasonality — Amazon above all — sequential growth
   is noise and YoY is the signal. This is the single most important number an analyst
   looks at and it isn't on the page.

---

## Part 0 — The two direct questions

### Column order still opens on the oldest period

Unfixed. The requirement is unchanged: chronological left-to-right, **opening scrolled to
the right-hand (newest) edge.**

`st.dataframe` has no scroll-position API, which is real. Three routes remain, in order of
preference:

1. `direction: rtl` on the scroll container with `direction: ltr` on the inner content —
   the standard CSS trick for opening a horizontal scroller at its right edge. The open
   question is whether an injected `<style>` block survives Streamlit's sanitiser. Inline
   `style` attributes demonstrably don't; a `<style>` tag may.
2. A different table element that exposes scroll position.
3. Reverse the columns (newest leftmost) — the fallback, and only if 1 and 2 both fail.

**This has now cost three rounds.** Settle it definitively: try 1, and if it fails, say so
plainly and implement 3 in the same pass rather than reporting back.

### The cash flow statement is five lines, and that is not a cash flow statement

Currently: cash from operations, capex, free cash flow, stock-based compensation,
depreciation & amortisation. That is a cash-flow *summary*, and free cash flow is the only
thing on it you can't get elsewhere.

A cash flow statement has three sections, and the missing ones carry information nothing
else on this dashboard does:

| Section | Missing lines that matter |
|---|---|
| Operating | working-capital movements (receivables, inventory, payables), deferred taxes, non-cash adjustments other than D&A and SBC |
| **Investing** | acquisitions, purchases/maturities of marketable securities, **purchases of equity investments** |
| **Financing** | **share repurchases**, **dividends paid**, debt issued, debt repaid, finance-lease principal payments |
| Reconciliation | effect of FX, net change in cash, beginning and ending cash |

The financing section is the sharpest omission. **You currently cannot see whether any of
these three companies bought back a single share.** For an equity dashboard that is a
first-order gap — buybacks are the main use of cash at Amazon and NVIDIA, and share count
is the denominator of everything an equity investor cares about.

The investing section matters for a second reason specific to this watchlist: Amazon's
purchases of Anthropic and OpenAI equity flow through investing activities. The dashboard
currently reports the *balance-sheet* consequence of those investments and none of the cash
movement.

## Part 1 — Defects

### D12 — Q4 is missing from the income statement and balance sheet

**Severity: highest in this review.**

Verified against `xbrl_facts`: at `period_end = 2024-12-31`, Amazon's only revenue fact is
the 365-day figure. There is no three-month Q4 fact, for any company, in any year. The same
is true of NVIDIA at every January period end.

The result is visible in the tables: AMZN's income statement shows `—` at Dec 31 columns;
NVIDIA's shows `—` at every January column. Revenue, cost of goods sold, operating income,
pre-tax income, net income — one quarter in four, blank, on the primary statement.

**The fix already exists.** It is exactly the derivation built for cash flow: `Q4 = FY − 9M`,
same fiscal-boundary logic, same fail-closed rule, same derived-cell marking. It was scoped
to cash flow when the problem is general.

Apply it to every duration-based line on the income statement. The balance sheet is
unaffected (instants, no subtraction needed).

**Test:** for each company and fiscal year, the four discrete income-statement quarters must
sum to the filed FY figure — the same sum-back rule already added for cash flow.

### D13 — No year-over-year comparison

The only growth shown is sequential. For Amazon this is close to useless: Q4 always jumps
and Q1 always falls, so `-13.6%` in Q1 tells you nothing about the business. Every
professional platform leads with YoY for quarterly data and offers sequential as secondary.

This is not a small addition — it is arguably the most valuable single number that could be
added to the Financials page.

Add a **YoY growth row** alongside (or as a toggle against) the sequential one. YoY compares
each quarter to the same quarter one fiscal year earlier, which the `filings.fiscal_year` /
`fiscal_period` machinery built for the discrete-quarter work already answers directly.

### D14 — Growth percentages on near-zero and sign-crossing bases are meaningless

Micron's quarterly cash flow currently shows `+4097.2%`, `+1578.8%`, `-397.4%`, `+340.0%`.

Two distinct problems:

- **Near-zero base.** Free cash flow moving from 72 to 3,022 is a real change; expressing it
  as `+4097.2%` conveys nothing and crowds the column.
- **Sign crossing.** FCF going from `+72` to `-113` produces a percentage that is
  mathematically defined and analytically nonsense. You cannot express a move from profit to
  loss as a percentage change.

The convention every professional platform uses is **`n/m`** (not meaningful). Apply it when
the base is zero, when the sign flips, or when the absolute base is below a threshold
relative to the line's own typical magnitude. Document the threshold rather than picking one
silently.

### D15 — Interest expense of exactly 0 for Micron

Micron's Q3 FY26 income statement reads `Interest expense: 0`.

Micron carries billions in debt. An interest expense of exactly zero for a quarter is
almost certainly either fully capitalised interest or a tagging artifact — not a fact about
the world. This project's own rule is that zero and missing are different facts; a zero that
should be a null is the same error in the opposite direction.

Worth checking whether the tag genuinely reported 0 or whether a resolution step produced it.

### D16 — The balance sheet is labelled "three-month periods"

A balance sheet is an instant, not a duration. The header `$m, three-month periods` above a
balance sheet is conceptually wrong, and it's the sort of thing that tells a professional
reader the tool doesn't know what it's showing.

Use "as-of dates" for the balance sheet and keep the duration label for the income and cash
flow statements.

Related: `↳ Growth %` is the wrong word for a balance-sheet item. Stock figures **change**;
flow figures **grow**. Use "Change %" on the balance sheet.

### D17 — Findings are not sorted by severity

On the Filings page the order runs Medium, Medium, Low, Low, Medium, Low, Low, **High**,
Medium, Medium, Low, **High**, Medium, **High**, Low.

The three High findings — the $15.9B discrete tax expense, the Anthropic revaluation, the
Indian tax dispute — are scattered mid-list among fog-index observations. Sort descending by
severity, then by category.

### D18 — Brief and Observations duplicate each other on the Filings page

`Gross margin reached 52.3%, the highest level in 19 quarters` (Brief) and
`High — Gross margin of 52.3% is the highest in 19 quarters` (Observations) are the same
fact, one screen apart.

D10 fixed exactly this on the Overview's "What changed?" by excluding observations already
cited in a kept brief sentence. The same exclusion was never applied to the Filings page.

### D19 — Two of three chart series are the same colour

On the Metrics page, AMZN and MU are both blue and cannot be distinguished. Only NVDA is
separable. Series colour carries no severity meaning, so this doesn't touch the no-colour
rule — it's just a palette that needs three distinguishable values.

### D20 — Section text is raw and unreadable

The Sections viewer opens with `v3.26.1`, then the note title three times, then
`DISCLOSURESUnaudited Interim Financial Information` with words run together, then several
thousand words with no paragraph breaks.

These are artifacts of the SEC's R-file rendering. The text is correct — the analysis layer
reads it fine — but as something a person is expected to read it is unusable. At minimum:
strip the viewer version string and the repeated title, and restore paragraph breaks.

## Part 2 — What a professional needs and cannot get here

Ordered by how badly the absence is felt.

### Share count and per-share figures

`EarningsPerShareDiluted` and `WeightedAverageNumberOfDilutedSharesOutstanding` are **already
in the database** — the observations layer cites diluted EPS growth — but neither appears on
the income statement, and share count appears nowhere at all.

Without share count you cannot see dilution, cannot see the effect of buybacks, and cannot
compute a single per-share figure. For equity research this is the most conspicuous gap on
the page after Q4.

Add to the income statement: basic EPS, diluted EPS, basic shares, diluted shares.

### Segment data

Amazon is three businesses — North America, International, AWS — with completely different
margins, and consolidated operating margin is close to meaningless without the split. AWS is
the entire investment case. NVIDIA's Data Center vs Gaming split is the same story.

The Segment Information note is extracted and analysed as text. Its numbers are not parsed.
This is the largest single piece of missing *data* rather than missing presentation.

### Trailing twelve months

No TTM column anywhere. For any company with seasonality or lumpy quarters, TTM is the
standard way to read a trend. It is pure arithmetic over four stored discrete quarters —
which now exist for cash flow and, once D12 lands, will exist for the income statement too.

### Common-size statements

No "% of revenue" view on the income statement. One toggle; standard on every platform;
makes margin structure legible at a glance in a way absolute dollars never do.

### Balance sheet completeness

Missing and not derivable from what's shown: **total liabilities**, goodwill, intangibles,
retained earnings, short-term debt and current portion of long-term debt, operating lease
liabilities.

Total liabilities is the one that stings — the sheet currently shows total assets and total
equity but not the number between them.

### Valuation

There is no share price, market cap, or any multiple. This is presumably deliberate — the
project is built on filings only, and prices aren't in EDGAR — but it should be a stated
scope decision rather than a silent absence, because "equity research dashboard" implies it.
Share count (above) at least makes per-share analysis possible.

### Non-operating income share

The Amazon Q2 2026 filing reports **$53.4B of other income against $27.5B of operating
income** — the majority of reported profit is an unrealised Level 3 mark on private
companies. Nothing on the dashboard surfaces this. Beneish won't catch it; the margin
metrics actively obscure it, because net margin of 31.2% looks like an operating triumph.

A single derived check — non-operating income as a share of pre-tax income, flagged above a
threshold — would have caught the most important thing in that filing.

## Part 3 — Presentation

Individually small, collectively the difference between "internal tool" and "product".

1. **Column headers are truncated mid-date**: `Jun 30, 202`, `Dec 31, 202`. Unreadable and
   ambiguous.
2. **No fiscal labels.** A professional reads `Q3 FY26`, not `May 28, 2026`. For Micron,
   whose fiscal year ends in late August/early September, calendar dates are actively
   misleading — `Sep 3, 2020` and `Aug 29, 2019` are the same fiscal quarter a year apart
   and nothing on screen says so. Label columns `Q3 FY26` with the calendar date secondary.
3. **Line-item labels truncate**: `Property, plant and equipment and…`.
4. **Numbers are left-aligned.** Financial tables right-align numerals so digits line up by
   place value. This is the single cheapest change with the largest effect on how the table
   reads.
5. **Negatives use a minus sign.** Finance convention is parentheses: `(8,821)`.
6. **Growth rows are full-height**, doubling the table's vertical size. They should be
   visually subordinate — smaller, tighter, clearly a sub-row.
7. **The Plotly toolbar overlaps the "Operating margin" chart title** on the Overview.
8. **No data-freshness indicator** except one vague line on Filings ("Data as of the current
   deployment's database"). Every page should state the latest filing date it reflects.
9. **Metrics page is one chart per metric, stacked.** Forty-five metrics is a lot of
   scrolling; a small-multiples grid would show a category at a glance.
10. **No export.** `st.dataframe`'s download button exists but is buried in a hover toolbar.

## Suggested order

**First — the numbers are wrong or missing:**

1. D12 — Q4 on the income statement. A quarter of the primary statement.
2. D13 — year-over-year growth.
3. D14 — `n/m` for meaningless percentages.
4. D15 — Micron's zero interest expense.

**Second — the statements are incomplete:**

5. Full three-section cash flow statement.
6. EPS and share counts on the income statement.
7. Balance sheet completeness (total liabilities first).

**Third — readability, which is cheap and high-leverage:**

8. Right-align numbers, parentheses for negatives, fiscal column labels, untruncated
   headers.
9. Column scroll position (settle it).
10. D16, D17, D18, D19 — labels, sorting, dedupe, chart colours.

**Fourth — larger analytical additions:**

11. TTM columns.
12. Common-size toggle.
13. Non-operating income flag.
14. Segment data — the biggest piece of work here, and the biggest payoff.

**Last:** C1's visual pass, over a layout that has stopped moving.

## One note on sequencing

Items 1–7 are data problems and they are what actually make this feel unprofessional. A
beautifully styled table that is missing every Q4 and cannot show year-over-year growth will
still not survive five minutes with someone who does this for a living. The styling work is
worth doing — it just isn't what's wrong.
